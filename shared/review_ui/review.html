Coding guide (INEL / GRAID conventions)
SSubject of a main-clause predicate (verbal, nominal, adjectival, pronominal or particle predicate). Tag the head of the phrase only (usually its last word).
ODirect object of a main-clause predicate, whatever its case: accusative, partitive or unmarked. Tag the head only.
NAnything else: modifiers inside a phrase, possessors, adverbials, predicates, and all words inside subordinate clauses (converb, participle, relative, complement, temporal, conditional, purpose clauses). INEL tags arguments only in main clauses.
UYou cannot decide. Add a note. Unsure items are discussed together.

Decide from the meaning (translation and glosses), not from the case ending: an unmarked noun can be an object. Dolgan: what to look for

Counts below are from the 50 texts INEL tagged by hand (Dolgan Corpus 2.0). The gloss line is the second line under each word.

Objects (O).

  • -ACC (accusative) is the usual object marking: fish-ACC. About 516 tagged objects end like this.
  • Possessed objects carry a person label before the accusative: father-3SG-ACC "his/her father", house-1SG-ACC "my house". About 440 objects look like this. The person label here is the possessor, not the subject.
  • Unmarked objects end in [NOM] (zero ending, in brackets), the same as subjects: about 178 tagged objects, roughly 1 in 7. These are the hardest cases: decide by meaning. Ask "who does what to what?" using the translation.
  • -PART (partitive) objects are rare (23 tagged), mostly in commands, future and conditional clauses ("give (some) tea!").
  • Plural objects: -PL-ACC, -PL-3SG-ACC.
  • Other cases are not direct objects: DAT/LOC (to, at; Dolgan has one case for both), ABL (from), INSTR (with, by means of), COM (together with), COMP (than). Tag these N.

Subjects (S).

  • Subjects are almost always unmarked: man.[NOM], reindeer-PL.[NOM].
  • -3SG.[NOM] on a noun means "his/her X" as a subject: again the person label is the possessor.
  • Personal pronouns (part of speech pers) carry their own person label. A pronoun subject is S like any other.
  • Dolgan drops subjects freely: the verb ending (go-PST1-3SG) already says who. Many utterances have no overt subject. Never tag an object or adverb as S to fill the gap.
  • Clauses without a verb still have subjects: "the tent [is] big", "there is no fish". The predicate is then a noun, adjective, or the negative particle hu͡ok, often with a bracketed person ending such as .[3SG] or with a copula (cop). The noun it describes is S.

Subordinate clauses: everything inside them is N. INEL tags subjects and objects only in main clauses. In Dolgan, dependent clauses are mostly built on non-finite verbs. Look at the verb that follows the noun:

  • CVB = converb ("having done X", "while doing X"): CVB.SEQ, CVB.SIM, CVB.ANT, CVB.PURP (in order to). The most common subordinate verbs in the tagged texts.
  • PTCP = participle: PTCP.PRS, PTCP.PST, PTCP.FUT. Heads relative clauses ("the man who came") and complement clauses ("I know that he will die"). A participle with its own case ending (die-PTCP.FUT-1SG-ACC) is a whole clause used as an argument: tag its words N.
  • COND = conditional ("if ...").
  • A noun in front of a converb is usually that converb's argument, so N.
  • But INEL is not uniform here. Of accusative nouns directly in front of a converb, about a third are tagged O in the INEL texts. The likely reason is that a converb plus a main verb can describe one action ("took it and left"). Working rule: if converb and main verb are one action, O; if the converb clause is a separate event, N; if you cannot tell, U with a note. This is the hardest call in Dolgan, and calibration will show how close our rule is to INEL's practice.

Other things to keep in mind.

  • Word order is mostly subject–object–verb, but nouns after the verb occur. A noun after the verb can still be S or O.
  • Tag only the head of a phrase: in "this big tent-ACC", only "tent" is O; "this" and "big" are N.
  • Russian words are tagged like Dolgan words. They sometimes appear without Dolgan endings, so judge them by meaning.
