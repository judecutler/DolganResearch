**Read on 2026-11-05 (web Claude, WebFetch of the full text; WebFetch returns a model's extraction, so check each quote against the PDF before citing):** Däbritz (2023, JEL), p. 196: "There is only one dedicated evidential item, namely the inferential {-DA4K} + AGR.POSS-suffixes"; "some tense-aspect forms express evidentiality concomitantly, most prominently the postterminal {-BI4T}"; "the third-person singular postterminal form of the copula ä- has developed into an evidential particle" (glossed EVID, ex. 55, spelled *äbit*). pp. 195-196 also name a "hypothetical mood formed with {-DA4K}", the same shape as the inferential, which fits the corpus glossing `TAK` as both `COND1` and `INFER`. **What this changes for Study A**
- The Evenki literature describes indirect evidentiality mainly through the **perfect**, not through a dedicated `-rkə` suffix. The subagent found no source naming `-rkə`, yet the INEL Evenki corpus glosses 29 tokens of `rkə` as `EVID`. Before comparing, check Nedjalkov (1997) and Bulatova and Grenoble (1999) for this form, and include the Evenki perfect (`PRF`, 110 tokens in 002) among the Evenki candidate forms (D-007).
- The contribution is now narrower: **quantitative and corpus-based**. The qualitative comparison and the contact claim exist in Skribnik and Aikhenvald (2024). Frame the paper as testing their description against corpus data: does Dolgan's lack of reportatives hold? How often are `-TAK` and `e:t` used, by genre and period? Do Dolgan and Evenki use their indirect forms in similar contexts at similar rates? Cite Hagel (2022) as the INEL corpus-method precedent.
- Skribnik and Aikhenvald (2024: 1018) answer two open data questions: **`eːt` is the contracted *e-bit***, and **`-TI` (corpus `PST1`) is the neutral past**, which makes `BIT`/`PST2` the indirective past (D-022, now supported by a source, though the corpus label itself is not discussed there).

**Rule:** do not pitch "differential object marking in Dolgan" as new. Pitch the age comparison.

**Read on 2026-11-05 (web Claude, WebFetch of the full text from digar.ee; quotes come from a model's extraction, so check them against the PDF before citing):** Siegl (2015), §5, pp. 269-271 and fn. 24. He claims that "the encoding of secondary predicates switched from dative to instrumental sometime in the 20th century" (p. 270), that Dolgan speakers initiated it late in the century, and that earlier texts show the dative. The evidence is two examples: an instrumental one from a published text, "AAB II 34" ('worked as an educator ... now works as a radio operator'), and a dative one from the folklore collection "DF 29: 328" ('was (as) a Russian sorcerer', with the copula *e-te*). **No counts, no criteria for identifying a secondary predicate, and no speaker or date information.** Fn. 24 cites Artemjev (1999: 81-82, 102-103), who also links the shift to Russian. **Artemjev (1999) is a new lead that must be checked**: it may already describe the variation. So the apparent-time and real-time test stays open: Siegl asserts the shift without counting.
